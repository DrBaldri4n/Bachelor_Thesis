This confirms that decision tree classifiers provide a powerful and efficient method for selecting features when deploying LSTM-based models.\\

As highlighted in both this work and related literature, flooding attacks can be detected with high reliability under comparable hardware configurations. However, the robustness of the detection results strongly depends on the underlying system and the specific hardware events that can be recorded, as well as on the similarity of their characteristics. If the hardware architecture differs significantly or if kernel-level event traces deviate, the model’s performance may degrade. This emphasizes the need for adaptive feature engineering and retraining when applying the model to new or heterogeneous environments.\\
Consequently, the model must be able to determine which hardware events are available and reliable on the target system, and to identify which features are most relevant for attack detection in that specific context. Since hardware performance counters vary across processor generations and architectures, it cannot be assumed that the same set of events will always be accessible or equally meaningful. This variability underlines the importance of a flexible and adaptive approach to feature selection when deploying the model on different hardware platforms~\cite{different_hardware_events}.\\

Another important limitation is the “black-box” nature of the LSTM model. As described in Chapter~2 \textit{Fundamentals – Overview of LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) Architecture}, the complex design composed of multiple gates and memory cells creates significant challenges in interpreting how these models arrive at specific decisions. This lack of interpretability makes it difficult to explain why particular patterns of hardware events are classified as attacks, especially under varying system conditions. As a result, the robustness and trustworthiness of the model may be reduced when deployed in real-world, diverse, or evolving scenarios.\\
This “black-box” problem manifests in several ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item LSTMs have an intricate internal structure that involves several nonlinear operations, which complicates the understanding of how input data is processed and decisions are made.  
    \item The model’s sensitivity to hyperparameters, potential overfitting, and training instability further increase the complexity of the interpretability challenge.  
    \item The standard outputs of LSTM models do not provide any explanation for the decisions made, which is a significant drawback in security-critical applications such as intrusion detection.  
\end{itemize}
In the \textit{Future Work}, we will discuss potential approaches to address these limitations, such as the application of explainable AI (XAI) techniques (e.g., saliency maps, LIME, or SHAP) or the use of simpler surrogate models that provide more transparency in their decision-making processes\cite{lstm_transparency}.
